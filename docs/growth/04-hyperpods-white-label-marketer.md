# 04 — HyperPods as a White-Label Marketer Tool

**One line:** You are not the DFY studio for the podcaster. You are the production
partner for the marketer who already owns the podcaster.

**The unit of sale is a roster, not an episode.** One podcast marketing agency with
10 clients = 10 recurring HyperPod contracts routed through a single relationship,
a single invoice, a single onboarding, and a single point of churn risk.

---

## 1. Why this play works

Podcast marketing agencies sit on an unserved gap. They own the client, the
strategy, the reporting, and the billing. What they do not own is a repeatable
visual production line. Today they either:

- **don't offer it** — the episode ships, the clips ship, and nothing carries the
  ideas into feeds where decision-makers actually stop scrolling; or
- **outsource it badly** — a rotating cast of Fiverr designers and Canva templates,
  which means inconsistent quality across the roster, unpredictable turnaround, and
  a margin that evaporates into account-management time.

The HyperPod pipeline is the exact opposite shape: a locked, staged production
system (intake → SVA → extraction → copy → visual grammar → design brief → executor
→ critique) that produces the same HP-9 artifact at the same quality on the same
clock, for any show, at volume. That is precisely the thing an agency cannot build
and can immediately resell.

### What each side brings

| The agency brings | You bring |
|---|---|
| The client relationship and trust | The production system |
| Strategy, positioning, reporting | Insight extraction and visual grammar |
| Briefing and expectation management | Consistent quality at volume |
| Billing, collections, retention | Turnaround guarantees |
| Distribution and posting | Per-client brand kits that hold across episodes |

### Why it beats selling direct

- **CAC amortizes across a roster.** One sale, N contracts. Sell the agency once,
  onboard clients 2–10 with near-zero acquisition cost.
- **The sales cycle collapses after the pilot.** Client #1 is a sale. Clients #2–10
  are an internal rollout decision the agency makes without you in the room.
- **The expensive part of DFY gets outsourced to them.** Client hand-holding, scope
  arguments, "can we make the logo bigger" — that's the agency's job now. You get a
  briefed, pre-filtered input and a single professional counterparty.
- **Retention rides on their retention.** Agencies keep clients on annual retainers.
  Episode-level buyers churn at episode level.

### What you give up (name it now, price it in)

- **Concentration risk.** One partner churning takes 10 contracts, not one.
- **Margin pressure.** The agency will squeeze wholesale every renewal.
- **Invisible attribution.** No logo, no case study, no inbound from the work itself.
- **No end-client signal.** You lose direct feedback on what actually performed.

These are real, and Section 9 prices and bounds each one. None of them is a reason
not to run the play; all of them are reasons to run it with a contract.

## 2. Who exactly to sell to

Ranked by fit, best first.

| Tier | Buyer | Why they buy | Roster size |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Podcast marketing / repurposing agencies | Already sell "episode → content." Visual is the missing SKU. | 8–40 clients |
| **A** | Podcast production studios moving upmarket | Have audio/video, want higher-margin strategy work to sell | 5–25 |
| **B** | B2B content agencies with 2+ podcast clients | Podcast is a service line, not a specialty — they need a partner, not a hire | 3–10 |
| **B** | Podcast networks and show collectives | Centralized ops, one decision-maker, homogeneous format | 10–60 shows |
| **C** | Fractional CMOs / solo operators with a book of clients | Fast yes, small volume, useful for early proof | 2–5 |
| **C** | PR and guest-booking agencies | Adjacent, need education on why visual matters | 10–50 |

**Disqualifiers.** Skip agencies that: sell one-off projects rather than retainers
(no recurring volume); have an in-house design team with spare capacity (you're a
threat, not a partner); serve clients whose episodes are interview fluff with no
extractable ideas (your SVA will reject half their roster and the relationship dies
on quality perception); or want to pay per-lead / rev-share instead of wholesale.

**Ideal partner profile:** 8+ retained podcast clients, publishing weekly, already
charging for repurposing, no in-house designer, and an account manager who is
visibly the bottleneck.

## 3. What "white-label" actually has to mean

White-label fails when it means "we'll remove our logo." It works when the agency
can run the whole thing as their own product line. Ship all eight of these or the
partner will leak work back to you as ad-hoc requests.

1. **Unbranded deliverables.** HP-9 SVG + PNG exports, no watermark, no filename
   giveaway. The end client sees the agency's brand, or their own, and nothing else.
2. **Per-client brand kits.** One `hv-brand-kit` per end client, versioned, so
   episode 40 looks like episode 1. This is the single biggest thing an agency
   cannot reproduce with freelancers, and the reason they stay.
3. **Async intake.** A submission form that maps straight to `EPISODE-BRIEF.json`,
   so an account manager can drop an episode at 11pm without booking a call.
   Synchronous intake does not survive volume.
4. **A written SLA.** Turnaround clock, revision policy, capacity commitment,
   escalation path. Agencies resell promises; give them a promise they can quote.
5. **Agency-branded sell collateral.** A sell sheet with a logo slot, 3 sample
   HyperPods, a "what your client receives" mockup, and a margin calculator. Their
   ability to resell is your revenue; do not leave it to them.
6. **A partner workspace.** Shared drive, one folder per end client, predictable
   file naming, delivery log. Their AM should never have to ask "where is it."
7. **Invisibility clause.** You never contact their client. Ever. In writing.
8. **A monthly roll-up.** Episodes delivered, per client, in a format they can paste
   into their own client report. This makes your work legible inside their QBR,
   which is where renewal decisions get made.

## 4. Pricing architecture

> **All figures below are illustrative placeholders.** Replace with your true
> numbers. What matters is the *structure* — the floor rule, the tier ladder, and
> the margin the agency can see.

### The floor rule

Never quote wholesale below **2.5× true unit cost**, where true unit cost includes
production time, the QA/critique pass, and an allowance for one revision round. If
a tier breaks that floor, don't discount — cut scope (fewer revision rounds, longer
SLA) instead. Discounting the same deliverable teaches the partner that your price
is a starting position.

### Wholesale ladder (per HyperPod, monthly committed volume)

| Tier | Pods / month | Wholesale | Agency resells at | Agency margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pilot | 1–4 | $450 | $900–1,200 | ~55% |
| Partner | 5–14 | $375 | $850–1,000 | ~58% |
| Scale | 15–39 | $310 | $800–950 | ~63% |
| Network | 40+ | $260 | $750–900 | ~68% |

The ladder does two jobs at once: it rewards roster penetration (their incentive to
sell clients #4–10 for you), and it makes the margin story better as they grow,
which is what keeps a partner from re-shopping the category.

### Additional line items

- **Client onboarding fee — $500 one-time per new end client.** Covers brand kit
  build and the first-episode calibration. Non-negotiable; it's the thing that makes
  episodes 2–40 cheap, and it stops agencies from cycling clients through you.
- **Monthly floor — the committed tier volume, billed whether used or not.** This is
  what turns a vendor relationship into reserved capacity. No floor, no SLA.
- **Rush (sub-48h) — +40%.** Available, capped per month, deliberately expensive.
- **Revisions — one round included** (executed against the `hyperpod-designer-brief`
  action log). Round two onward billed at a flat rate. Publish this on day one; it
  is the #1 source of white-label margin leakage.
- **SVA rejection — no charge, credited.** If an episode fails the viability gate,
  the agency pays nothing and gets a written reason they can forward to their client.
  Turning a reject into a consultative moment is a retention feature, not a refund.

### Worked example — one agency, one year

Assume a Tier-A partner: 10 clients, 4 episodes/month each = 40 pods/month.

| | Monthly | Annual |
|---|---|---|
| Your revenue @ $260 (Network tier) | $10,400 | $124,800 |
| Agency revenue @ $800 | $32,000 | $384,000 |
| Agency gross margin | $21,600 | $259,200 |

Plus $5,000 in one-time onboarding across the 10 clients.

**Sensitivity that matters more than the rate:** roster penetration. The same
partner at 3 clients (12 pods/mo @ $310) is $3,720/month. The rate card moved 16%;
the revenue moved 3×. Every hour you'd spend negotiating price is better spent
getting clients #4–10 activated. Build the whole partner motion around that number.

## 5. The SKUs the agency resells

Give the partner three things to sell, not one. A single SKU makes them a reseller;
three makes it a product line with their name on it.

**Episode HyperPod** — one HP-9 per episode, brand-kit consistent. The recurring core.

**Season Pack** — 8 or 12 episodes committed up front, delivered on a publishing
calendar. Sells better than per-episode because it matches how their client already
budgets, and it locks your capacity.

**Launch Kit** — for a new show or a relaunch: brand kit build, the first 3
HyperPods, plus a back-catalog pass on the 3 highest-performing legacy episodes.
Higher ticket, front-loaded, and it's the natural upsell when the agency wins a new
podcast client. This is the SKU that gets you into a roster on day one instead of
month six.

## 6. Delivery operations

The whole model rests on the production line being genuinely repeatable. Anything
you handle ad-hoc for one partner becomes an expectation for all of them.

**Per new end client (once):**
1. Brand kit build via `hv-brand-kit` — Path B from their existing collateral where
   possible, versioned and stored under the partner workspace.
2. Calibration episode — first HyperPod runs with an explicit approval step, so the
   agency's taste gets encoded into the kit rather than relitigated every month.
3. Kit lock. From here, episodes are production, not design.

**Per episode (steady state):**

| Stage | Skill | Gate |
|---|---|---|
| Intake | `hv-intake` | Async form → brief LOCKED |
| Viability | `hv-source-viability` | PROCEED / HOLD / REJECT (reject = credit) |
| Extraction | `hv-insights-extractor` | 9 viable insights or escalate |
| Copy | `hv-copy-engine` | COPY-PACKAGE locked |
| Visual grammar | `hv-visual-grammar` | Every block assigned |
| Brief | `hv-design-brief` | Three team briefs |
| Build | `hv-executor` | SVG + PNG |
| QA | `hyperpod-design-critique` | **Never ship without this pass** |
| Revisions | `hyperpod-designer-brief` | One round included |

**Batch by client, not by date.** Running an end client's episodes together reuses
the loaded brand kit and the extraction context, and it's where your unit-cost
advantage over the agency's freelancer bench actually comes from.

**The QA gate is the product.** The agency is buying "I never have to look at it
before it goes to my client." A single bad asset reaching an end client costs you
the roster, not the episode. `hyperpod-design-critique` runs on every pod, no
exceptions, no "it's just a small one."

**Capacity math before you sign.** Know your true pods/week ceiling and hold roughly
25% headroom for rush and revision load. Signing a 40-pod floor you can't hit is how
this play kills a business instead of building one.

## 7. The partner funnel

### Step 1 — Target list

Build a list of 60–100 agencies fitting the ideal partner profile. Sources: podcast
agency directories, LinkedIn (`podcast marketing agency`, `podcast repurposing`),
the client-logo walls of podcast hosting and editing tools, show credits in the
notes of B2B podcasts, and the Apollo filters for agencies with 5–50 employees in
marketing services. Qualify on roster size and retainer model before outreach.

### Step 2 — The spec pod (this is the entire pitch)

Pick one of their existing clients' recent episodes. Produce a real HyperPod from it,
fully branded to the *end client*, unbranded as to you. Send it to the agency owner
with roughly this:

> Subject: made this from [Client]'s ep. 47
>
> [Name] — I produce podcast-to-visual assets white-label for agencies. I made this
> from [Client]'s episode 47 so you can see the actual output rather than a deck.
> It's yours regardless — post it under your name if it's useful.
>
> The model: you resell it to your roster at your own price, I stay invisible, your
> client never knows I exist. Agencies typically run ~60% margin on it.
>
> Worth 15 minutes to see the rate card?

No deck, no calendar link in email one, no "quick question" opener. The asset is the
argument. Do not spec-produce for more than ~10 targets before you've closed one —
if the asset isn't converting, the target list is wrong, not the volume.

### Step 3 — Pilot

One client, two episodes, standard Pilot-tier pricing (discount the *scope*, not the
rate — e.g. waive the onboarding fee, not the per-pod price). Ten-day turnaround.
The goal of the pilot is a delivered asset the agency has already resold, not a
positive impression.

### Step 4 — Rollout

The moment pilot episode 2 ships, the ask is not "want to do more?" It is:

> "Which three clients go next?"

Roster penetration is the entire economic engine. Make expansion the default
conversation at every touchpoint, and give the agency a reason — the tier ladder —
to want it as badly as you do.

### Step 5 — Partner agreement

Non-negotiables in writing: white-label rights granted; **non-circumvention** (they
don't approach your other partners' clients, they don't rebuild the pipeline
in-house from your deliverables); **non-solicit both ways** (you never contact their
client; they never poach your team); payment terms (net 15, monthly floor billed in
advance); capacity reservation tied to the committed tier; revision and rush policy;
termination with 30 days' notice and brand kits released to them on exit — being
easy to leave is what makes them comfortable to start.

Grant **no category or territory exclusivity** in the first year. If a partner
demands it, the price is a volume floor that replaces the revenue you're forgoing.

## 8. Objection handling

**"How do I know quality stays consistent across ten clients?"**
Because it isn't a design service, it's a production line with gates. Locked format
(HP-9), a viability gate before we produce, a critique pass before we ship, and a
versioned brand kit per client. Here are three pods from three different shows —
notice they're consistent in structure and distinct in brand.

**"What if my client asks who made it?"**
You did. I'm under NDA, I never contact your client, and nothing I deliver carries
my name. That's the deal, in the contract.

**"What if an episode is a dud?"**
The viability assessment runs before production. If it fails, you pay nothing and
get a written reason you can forward — which is usually a better client conversation
than a mediocre asset would have been.

**"What's my actual margin?"**
[Rate card + calculator]. At your volume you're at $X wholesale reselling at $Y.
Most partners run 55–65%.

**"Turnaround?"**
Standard is [N] business days from intake to delivery, one revision round included.
Rush available at +40%, capped monthly so it stays real.

**"I don't have time to sell this."**
You don't have to build the pitch. You get a sell sheet with your logo, three sample
pods, and a spec pod produced for each of your top three clients. Your AM forwards
an email.

**"Why wouldn't I just hire a designer?"**
You could — at [salary] plus ramp, and you'd own the capacity problem when three
clients publish in the same week. I'm variable cost that flexes with your roster,
and I don't quit.

**"Can I brand the service as my own?"**
Yes. Name it, price it, put it in your deck. [Agency] Visual Lab. I'm plumbing.

## 9. Guardrails

| Risk | Guardrail |
|---|---|
| **Concentration** — one partner is 60% of revenue | Cap any single partner at ~35% of monthly revenue. When one approaches it, that's the trigger to sign partner #3, not to celebrate. |
| **Channel conflict** — your direct clients vs. theirs | Never quote a direct client below your partner's resale price in their market. Undercutting a partner once ends the channel permanently. Consider going direct-light and letting the channel own the category. |
| **Margin erosion** — annual squeeze | Hold the floor rule. Concede scope (SLA, revision rounds), never rate. Tier upgrades are the pressure valve — make them earn a lower rate with volume. |
| **Quality dilution at volume** | The critique gate is non-negotiable. If capacity forces a skipped QA pass, you're over capacity — raise price or stop selling until you aren't. |
| **Brand kit sprawl** | Version and store every kit centrally, one per end client. Fifty un-versioned kits is an unrunnable business. |
| **Disintermediation** | Non-circumvention clause, plus the practical moat: they have the deliverable, not the pipeline. Never ship editable source files or the brief packages. |
| **Invisible attribution** | Negotiate the right to reference *anonymized* work ("a top-20 B2B show") in your own marketing, and keep a private portfolio for partner sales. |

## 10. Metrics

Track these weekly. The starred one is the business.

- **★ Roster penetration** — activated clients ÷ partner's total clients. Target 40%
  by month 3 of a partnership, 70% by month 12.
- Partners signed / in pilot / active
- Pods delivered per partner per month
- Wholesale ARPU per partner
- Gross margin per pod (the floor-rule check)
- Cycle time: intake → delivery
- Revision rate (>25% means the brand kit calibration is failing, not the designer)
- SVA rejection rate by partner (>20% means you signed the wrong roster)
- Partner churn and revenue concentration

## 11. First 90 days

**Days 1–30 — Make it sellable.**
Build the white-label kit from Section 3: unbranded delivery spec, async intake form,
SLA document, partner agreement, sell sheet with logo slot, margin calculator.
Set the real rate card against true unit cost. Build the 60-agency target list.
Produce 3 portfolio HyperPods across visibly different brands.

**Days 31–60 — First partner.**
Spec-pod the top 10 targets. Aim for 3 conversations, 1 pilot. Run the pilot at full
production discipline — every gate, every time; the pilot is your process test as
much as their trust test. Ship, then immediately ask which three clients go next.

**Days 61–90 — Penetrate and repeat.**
Expand partner #1 from 1 client to 4+. Start partner #2 outreach before partner #1
is fully rolled out. Instrument the metrics above. Do the capacity math honestly and
decide, with numbers, whether the next constraint is sales or production.

**The 90-day success condition:** one partner with 4+ activated clients on a monthly
floor, and a second partner in pilot. Not revenue — penetration and repeatability.
Those are what tell you the channel compounds.

---

## When *not* to run this play

- If your production line still requires you personally at more than one gate per
  episode, white-label will break it. Fix repeatability first.
- If you have fewer than three portfolio pieces across distinct brands, you have
  nothing to sell an agency; produce them.
- If direct-to-podcaster is currently your fastest path to cash, run this in
  parallel and slowly — the channel pays off in month 4+, not month 1.
