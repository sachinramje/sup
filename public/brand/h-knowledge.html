The HyperVisuals Media Co. Round 07 — the H, carrying an idea

An H that
knows something.

Every H I've drawn so far has been two posts and a bar with a different haircut. None of them said anything. These three each carry a knowledge idea inside the letter's own anatomy — not applied to it, built from it.

First, the honest problem with last round.

I built your H faithfully to that reference sheet, and faithful was the mistake. That 45° chunk-and-facet language is gaming and esports — it's the visual dialect of teams, tournaments and energy drinks. Beautifully made, but it reads as fast and loud, which is the exact opposite of what you're selling.

So I've kept the part of it that's genuinely yours — the precision of the 45° cut — and thrown out the part that isn't: the squat, heavy, square proportions. These are drawn at a real letter's proportion, 76 wide to 100 tall. That single change does most of the work.

Your question: wordmark or symbol?

You don't have to choose — that's a master-brand structure, and it has a standard shape. You need both, doing different jobs.

The H — symbol
Avatars, app icons, favicons, merchandise, the stamp on every visual you ship. It's what rolls every Hyper brand into one asset.
HyperVisuals — wordmark
Anywhere you're being introduced: the site header, a deck cover, an invoice, a contract. Words do the introducing; symbols only work once people already know you.
H + Hyper/Pod — lockup
The two together, in a fixed relationship. This is what a sub-brand actually uses day to day.

The rule that keeps it honest: the symbol never appears alone until the wordmark has earned it. For the first year or two, lock them together almost everywhere.

The recommendation — Codex H

A column, a stack of pages, an archive

The piers are fluted — incised with fine vertical channels, the way a classical column is. That one device reads three ways at once, and all three are yours.

A fluted column is the oldest signal of learning there is: the portico, the library, the academy. Seen flatter, those same lines are a stack of pages viewed edge-on. Flatter still, they're lines of text.

And the crossbar stays solid. Many sources, one connection — which is the entire business, stated in geometry rather than in a tagline.

01

Codex H

Idea — the archive

Fluted piers — column, pages, lines of text
Solid crossbar — the one connection
45° terminals — cut, not drafted

Nobody is doing fluting. That's the entire opportunity.

Every modern monogram is either a solid slab or a monoline stroke. Incised channels sit in neither camp — they belong to architecture and to books, which is precisely the territory you're claiming and precisely where no tech brand is looking.

It also solves something none of my earlier marks did: it gives you a texture. That flute rhythm extends to a pattern, a divider, a lower-third, a book edge on a cover — an identity element that isn't the logo but is unmistakably from the same hand.

Keep the channels fine. At two or three units they read as incision; much wider and the pier falls apart into a fence. The small-size cut fills them in entirely.

02

Span H

Idea — the connection

Two unequal piers — two unlike things
One bar — the only reason they're a letter

An H is two separate uprights that only become a letter because something joins them.

That is what an insight is. Two things nobody had put together, and the connection between them. The letter was already carrying the idea; it just needed to stop hiding it.

So the piers are deliberately unequal — heavy on the left, light on the right — because equal piers read as symmetry and symmetry reads as decoration. Unequal, they read as two different things, held. And the bar overshoots the lighter pier: the connection outlives what it connected.

The most conceptually exact of the three and the quietest. If you want a mark that rewards being explained once and then never needs explaining again, this is it.

03

Ruled H

Idea — the line you read along

Doubled crossbar — two rules, one slot
The slot — where the text would sit

Two fine rules with a gap between them. The meaning is in the space, not the marks.

It's ruled paper, a musical staff, the guides a scribe drew before writing a single word. Every one of those is an instrument for holding knowledge that is itself empty — which is a more interesting claim about wisdom than any solid shape can make.

Practically it's the sharpest of the three: two thin horizontals against two heavy verticals gives real contrast, and contrast is what makes a mark legible at a glance. It's also the most likely to be mistaken for an equals sign inside an H, so the slot has to stay narrower than the rules are thick.

The three, together

Same proportion, same 45° terminals, same weight. Only the idea inside changes.

Rolled into every Hyper brand

One H, every product. This is the structure you described — and the reason the symbol is worth the investment.

At the sizes people meet you

Podcast tile, avatar, list row, favicon. Watch Codex's flutes — below about 32px they close, which is exactly why it ships with a solid sibling.

Recommendation — Codex H

The one nobody in your market is drawing

You asked for something different from what's out there. Fluting is genuinely absent from contemporary identity — the whole field has spent a decade choosing between solid slabs and monoline strokes, and incised channels belong to neither. That's not novelty for its own sake; it's the visual language of libraries and books, which is the exact territory you're claiming.

Span is the more rigorous idea and I'd happily defend it. But it needs a sentence of explanation, and Codex doesn't — a fluted column reads as knowledge before anyone thinks about why. For a symbol that has to carry four brands, the one that needs no explanation wins.

Take Ruled's doubled bar as the layout device regardless of which mark you pick — two fine rules with a slot between them, holding headlines, captions and lower-thirds across everything you make.
