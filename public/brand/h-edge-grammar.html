Cut, not
drafted.

You were right about the edges. Everything I'd drawn met at 90° — that's a diagram, not a monogram. What separates a real mark from a rectangle arrangement is a rule about how every edge terminates. Here are three, and one of them is the answer.

The thing that was missing.

A monogram isn't a letter shape — it's a letter shape plus a terminal grammar: a single, ruthlessly consistent rule for how every stroke ends. That rule is what your eye registers as "designed," and it's what carries across a whole system when you extend to a typeface, a sub-brand, a pattern.

Without it, you get what I gave you: correct proportions and dead edges.

The recommendation — Nib H

One angle, cut through everything

Every terminal in this mark is cut at the same angle, as though one chisel passed through the whole letter without ever changing its grip.

That's not a style. It's the oldest rule in Western letterforms — Roman inscriptional carving, then the broad-nib pen, then every serif face descended from them. The horizontal is thinner than the verticals for the same reason: a blade held at an angle simply cuts less material going sideways.

Which gives you the thing you actually asked for. Legacy is not a look you apply — it's a construction rule that has already survived two thousand years. Run it through hard geometry and no curves and it reads as new.

01

Nib

Rule — one constant cutting angle

Every terminal — cut at one angle
The crossbar — thinner, because the blade says so

Change the angle and the crossbar's weight changes with it, because both come from the same number.

That's the test of a real grammar rather than a decoration: the contrast between thick and thin isn't a taste decision you defend in a meeting, it's a consequence. Drag the angle and watch the horizontal thin out on its own.

The contrast slider is the one concession to legibility — pure blade geometry makes the crossbar too fine to survive a favicon, so it blends back toward even weight. Keep it low. Everything interesting about the mark lives in the first quarter of that slider.

Look at the corner zooms above. That's what an edge is supposed to do — arrive somewhere, at an angle, on purpose.

02

Chamfer

Rule — corners are facets

Corners removed — at 45°, by rule
Which corners — is the whole design decision

This is the language of the sheet you sent me, built as a system instead of a drawing.

Chamfering everything is the obvious move and the weakest one — it reads as a bevel filter. The interesting settings are the selective ones. Diagonal cuts only the top-left and bottom-right, which puts rotation into a mark that never leans. Counters cuts only the four inside corners, so the letter stays square outside and gets sharp where the eye actually lingers.

Faceting is also the most literal expression of intelligence-as-cut-material: a rough stone and a cut stone differ only in edges, and only one of them is worth something.

03

Radius

Rule — outer soft, inner sharp

Outer corners — generously rounded
Inner corners — left sharp, or nearly

Rounding everything makes a mark friendly. Rounding only the outside makes it feel manufactured.

This is the industrial-design move — the one that reads as a machined part or a well-made object. Soft where a hand would touch it, sharp where the material was cut. The two radii should never match; the moment they do, it collapses into an app icon.

I'm including it because it's the most immediately likeable of the three and it will test best with people who aren't designers. It's also the least ownable — warmth is the most crowded space in modern identity, and this is the direction most likely to look like something else in two years.

The three edges, side by side

Same proportions, same weight, same letter. The only variable is what happens in the last two percent of every stroke — which is the entire difference between a mark you'd register and a mark you'd redraw.

Carried across the brands

Pick an edge grammar and watch it hold. The H never changes between products — only the word after the slash. And because the grammar is a rule rather than a shape, it extends to the wordmark, the sub-brand type, and eventually a full display face.

Where it actually lives

Knocked out of a filled tile, at the sizes people meet you: podcast art, avatar, list row, favicon. Watch the edge survive — or not. This is where Radius flatters itself and Nib earns its keep.

Recommendation — 01 Nib

The rule that outlives the trend

Chamfer is the most now, and that's precisely the problem — the sheet you sent me is a genre currently at peak supply, and marks built to look current are the ones that date hardest. Radius is the safest and the least yours.

Nib is the only one of the three whose logic predates the trend cycle entirely. A constant cutting angle produced the Roman capital, the humanist page, and every serif you've ever read. Building on it isn't nostalgia — it's picking the one rule that's already proven it survives. Cut with flat planes and no curves, it doesn't look historical at all; it looks like something made now by someone who knew what they were doing.

And it gives you the thing none of the others do: an angle you can name. Lock it, and it governs the mark, the wordmark's terminals, the sub-brand type, the pattern, the motion. One number the entire identity inherits.

What I'd do next:

  1. Trademark search before anything else — single letters are the most heavily registered class there is.
  2. Lock the nib angle. Then draw HYPER in the same grammar: every terminal in the wordmark cut at that identical angle. That's when it stops being a logo and becomes an identity.
  3. Draw the small-size variant — angle held, contrast raised, so the crossbar survives 16px. Ship it as an optical size and switch below ~24px.
  4. Optical correction pass at final scale: the angled terminals will need the piers a hair narrower than the maths says, because slanted cuts read heavier than flat ones.
