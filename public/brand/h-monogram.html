The HyperVisuals Media Co. Identity — Round 04 Master mark — H

One letter,
every brand.

HyperPod. HyperLibrary. HyperBrand. The word is the constant, so the letter is the mark — and unlike a symbol, it arrives already legible. Four ways to cut an H, and the system that hangs off whichever one wins.

Why this beats every abstract mark I've shown you.

A symbol has to be taught, once per brand, at your expense. A letter is read on sight — and yours is already in every product name you own. That makes the H the cheapest possible mark to launch and the hardest to dislodge later.

It also solves the problem that killed Nine: an H cannot be broken by a format count, a new product line, or a pivot in what you make. It's tied to the name, and the name isn't changing.

01

Cut H

Variable — the kerf

Two piers — untouched
One crossbar — cut free, floating in the gap

The crossbar doesn't touch the piers. The H is held together by your eye, not by the geometry.

This is The Cut and the monogram folded into one mark. The kerf — that clean gap of separation — is the same device, so the logic of the logo and the logic of your product are the same logic. That's the tightest identity on the table.

It reads instantly as H because closure is automatic; the brain refuses to see three shapes when it can see a letter. That half-second of assembly is the mark doing work. Push the kerf past about 6 and it starts to fall apart — find the edge and stop just short of it.

02

Monolith H

Variable — the taper

Heavy piers — mass, not line
Slot counters — widening as they descend

Piers thick enough to be walls, and two slots of light between them. Seen from slightly below, like everything built to outlast you.

The counters widen toward the bottom, which is the whole trick: it puts you underneath the mark looking up. That's the posture of a monument, and it's the only one of the four that has genuine stillness — nothing here is moving or resolving, it's just standing.

Set the crossbar low, as it is by default. A centred crossbar is neutral; a low one has a base, and things with bases read as permanent. This is the wisdom register taken literally, and it's the strongest of the four at very small sizes because it's almost entirely mass.

03

Gate H

Variable — the perspective

Two posts, one lintel — already a doorway
Far pier narrows — so you're looking through it

An H is two posts and a beam. It's a gate that happens to be a letter.

Taper the lintel toward the far pier and narrow that pier to match, and the letter stops being flat — the eye reads a threshold rather than a glyph. Pull taper to zero and it collapses into an ordinary H, which is the test that the perspective is load-bearing and not decoration.

The risk to watch: timid taper reads as a printing error rather than a decision. If you take this one, commit past 12 or don't do it at all.

04

Rule H

Variable — the overshoot

Crossbar overshoots — past both piers
The overshoot is a rule — it can run any length

The crossbar breaks out of the letter and keeps going. The mark and the layout are the same object.

That escaping bar is a horizontal rule you can extend to any length — a divider, an underline for a headline, the spine a caption hangs from, the line a lower-third rides in on. It's the same asset as the slash: not a picture, a rule that extends into everything you make later.

For a library and an archive that's the right instinct — a ruled line is what a record looks like. The cost is that this H is never square, so it needs its own clear-space spec and it will fight small square avatars. Use the plain H there and the ruled H everywhere you control the layout.

The system it hangs off

Pick a mark and watch the family. The H never changes between brands — only the word after the slash does. That discipline is what makes an H worth owning: three products, one impression, no re-teaching.

Where it actually lives

Podcast tiles, avatars, favicons — every platform wants a filled square, so the working icon is the H knocked out of a solid. This is the test that matters most, because it's the size most people will ever see you at.

Recommendation — 01 Cut H, with 04 as its layout rule

Take the kerf, keep the overshoot

Cut H wins on the argument I've been making since round one: it's the only mark where the logo's construction and the product's construction are the same idea. The gap that separates the crossbar from the piers is the gap that separates one insight from the next. You don't have to explain that — it just quietly holds.

Then take Rule H's overshoot and make it the layout device rather than a competing logo. Same crossbar, extended. One mark, one rule — the H for identity, the extended bar for every layout it has to live in.

Monolith is the one to keep in reserve. If Cut H tests as too quiet at very small sizes, Monolith is the answer, and it's the only one of the four that can be scaled down indefinitely without care.

What I'd do next:

  1. Trademark search on the chosen H, classes covering media production and software. Single letters are heavily registered — this is the step most likely to force a change, so do it before anything else.
  2. Lock the kerf as a ratio of pier width, not an absolute — that's the number the whole system inherits.
  3. Draw a small-size variant with the kerf widened, the way a typeface ships an optical size. Below roughly 24px the logo should switch to it automatically.
  4. Set the clear space off the pier width, and the wordmark's cap height off the crossbar height, so every lockup derives from the mark instead of being eyeballed.
